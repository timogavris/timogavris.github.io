What is a quantum dot?

A quantum dot is a tiny crystal of semiconductor, usually 2 to 10 nm across, made of a few hundred to a few thousand atoms. The ones on this page are cadmium selenide (CdSe). In the lab they are grown in hot solution and coated with a thin layer of organic molecules (ligands), so they float in a liquid like ink. That's what is in the vials.

What makes them special is that they are smaller than the natural size of an electron's wave in the material. The electron is trapped in all three directions, so instead of the continuous energy bands of a normal crystal, the dot has separate, sharp energy levels, like a single atom. That's why they are often called artificial atoms. Unlike a real atom, though, you can choose where the levels sit just by choosing the size.

"Quantum" is because quantum mechanics sets their properties. "Dot" is because they are confined in every direction, so they act as if they had zero dimensions:

Quantum wellA thin film: trapped in 1 direction, free in 2
Quantum wireA nanowire: trapped in 2 directions, free in 1
Quantum dotA nanocrystal: trapped in all 3 directions

Quantum dots are used in QLED TV screens, as glowing labels for imaging cells, and in research on solar cells and LEDs. The 2023 Nobel Prize in Chemistry went to Moungi Bawendi, Louis Brus and Alexei Ekimov for discovering and developing them. It is the same Brus as the equation further down.

What's happening at this size

How a dot glows under a UV lamp

  1. A UV photon carries more energy (about 3.4 eV at 365 nm) than any of these band gaps, so every dot in the vial absorbs it, whatever its size.
  2. The photon lifts an electron from the valence band into the conduction band, leaving a positive "hole" behind. Both quickly lose their leftover energy as heat and settle into the lowest levels, called 1Se and 1Sh.
  3. The electron drops back across the gap and gives out one photon. Its energy equals the gap, and the gap is set by size, so size alone picks the colour.

Why smaller means bluer

In a big crystal, the electron and hole made by a photon spread out over a large region. In CdSe this electron–hole pair (an exciton) is naturally about 11 nm across. Make the crystal smaller than that and the electron's wave is forced to fit between the walls of the dot.

It works like a guitar string: a shorter string plays a higher note, and a squeezed electron has more energy. That extra energy grows as 1/D², so halving the diameter makes it about four times bigger. More energy across the gap means a bluer photon.

The Brus equation (1984)

Egap = Ebulk + h²/(8R²)·(1/me* + 1/mh*) − 1.786·e²/(4πε₀εrR)
The middle term is the squeeze, which pushes the energy up. The last term is the electron and hole attracting each other, which pulls it down a little. A third, very small term (about 0.003 eV) is left out.

The simple model overshoots for small dots

There are two main reasons. Brus treats the dot's walls as infinitely high, but a real electron leaks slightly into its surroundings, which lowers its energy. It also assumes the electron behaves the way it does in bulk CdSe (the same "effective mass"), and that stops being true when the dot is only a few atomic layers wide.

So in the lab, chemists use measured calibration curves instead. You measure the absorption peak of a batch and read the size off the curve. This tool uses the curve from Yu et al. (2003) for every colour you see.

Why the glow has a width

One dot gives out a fairly narrow line of colour, about 60 meV wide at room temperature (dashed curve). But a vial holds trillions of dots, and no synthesis makes them all exactly the same size. Each size glows a slightly different colour, so what you see is all those lines added together.

Small dots spread out more for the same percentage spread, because their colour changes faster with size. Displays that use quantum dots want this peak as narrow as possible, since a narrower peak gives a purer colour.
